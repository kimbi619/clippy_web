The best way to get meeting notes from a WhatsApp call is to stop asking a person to take them. Give the call a clear shape — a goal, a short agenda, a recap at the end — and let an assistant like Clippy write the transcript and summary while everyone stays in the conversation. Then share the summary in the group chat while the call is still fresh.

This guide covers why notes from WhatsApp calls tend to go missing, how to run a call that produces good notes, and what to do with them afterwards.

## Why WhatsApp calls rarely produce notes

Plenty of teams run their week on WhatsApp group calls: a shop owner and their suppliers, a site manager and a crew, a volunteer committee, a family planning an event. These calls are quick to start because everyone already has the app. But they have three habits that make notes unlikely:

- **They start on a whim.** There's no calendar invite, so there's no agenda, and nobody is assigned to take notes.
- **People join from anywhere.** On a phone, in a car, at a counter. Taking notes in those places is hard.
- **The note-taker can't take part.** Whoever writes things down stops contributing, and their notes cover what they managed to catch, not necessarily what mattered.

The result is familiar. A week later, two people remember the decision differently, and there's nothing written down to settle it.

## Let the notes write themselves

WhatsApp doesn't record or transcribe calls itself, so you need help from outside the app. (Our guide on [how to transcribe a WhatsApp call](/blog/how-to-transcribe-whatsapp-calls) compares the options.) The approach we built Clippy around is to add the note-taker as a participant:

1. Add Clippy to the call like any contact. Only people on your Allowed callers list can do this.
2. Clippy announces itself so everyone knows it's there.
3. It writes a live transcript, labelled by speaker, that you can follow in the Clippy app.
4. When the call ends, it does a second, more accurate pass and writes a summary.

Nobody has to divide their attention between talking and typing. And because the transcript shows who said what, you can check the exact wording when it matters.

## How to run a call that produces good notes

An assistant can only summarise what was actually said. A call with a clear shape produces a clear summary; a call that wanders produces a summary of wandering. These habits help with or without software.

### Say the goal out loud at the start

Open with one sentence about what the call is for: "We need to agree the menu and who's buying what for Saturday." This helps people stay on topic, and it gives the summary an obvious frame.

### Name the topics in order

If there are several things to cover, list them: "Three things — the menu, the budget, and transport." When you move on, say so: "OK, budget." Clear transitions make the transcript easy to scan and the summary easy to follow.

### Make decisions explicit

Most confusion after a call comes from things that sounded agreed but never were. When something is decided, say it as a decision: "So we're going with the second supplier." If it isn't decided, say that too: "We'll decide on Thursday once we have the quote."

### Give every action an owner and a date

"Someone should call the venue" usually means nobody does. "Amina calls the venue by Wednesday" gets done. Saying the name and the day out loud means they end up in the transcript and the summary.

### Recap in the last minute

Before hanging up, go over the decisions and actions in a few sentences. It catches misunderstandings while everyone is still there to correct them. You can also ask Clippy directly: "Clippy, what have we agreed so far?" It answers out loud, from what has been said on the call.

### Mind the audio

Transcripts depend on audio quality. Ask people to mute when they aren't talking, use earphones instead of speakerphone, and avoid talking over each other. If the audio is too poor to transcribe reliably, Clippy tells you so rather than guessing — but it's better not to get there.

## What a useful summary contains

Whether Clippy writes it or you do, a good call summary is short and structured. It usually answers four questions:

- **What was the call about?** One or two lines.
- **What was decided?** A short list, in plain words.
- **Who is doing what, and by when?** Each action with a name and a date.
- **What's still open?** Questions that need an answer before the next call.

A transcript is the full record; a summary is what people actually read. Keep both: the summary for everyday use, the transcript for checking exactly what was said.

## After the call

### Share the summary where the group already is

The summary is most useful in the group chat, within minutes of hanging up. Take the summary from the Calls tab in the Clippy app and post it in the group. People who missed the call catch up in thirty seconds, and people who were there can correct anything that's wrong.

### Check the actions, not every word

You don't need to read the whole transcript after every call. Check that the decisions and actions in the summary are right. Go to the transcript only when there's a question about exactly what someone said.

### Start the next call from the last one

Open the next call by reading the open questions and actions from the last summary. It takes a minute and makes sure nothing quietly drops off.

## A word on consent

Notes from a call are a record of other people's words. Tell everyone at the start that Clippy is transcribing, and stop if someone objects. Rules on recording conversations differ between countries, and some require everyone's agreement. Clippy's announcement helps, but saying it yourself is clearer.
